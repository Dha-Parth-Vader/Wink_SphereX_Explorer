What would SPHEREx see?

Hide a made-up planet in real SPHEREx pictures, then try to find it by blinking. Its brightness and motion come from physics, and the detection limit comes from each picture's own noise. Often the honest answer is “you can't see it”.

What to hide

Earth is 1 AU from the Sun, Neptune 30 AU. Planet Nine, if it exists, could be 400 to 800 AU away.

Far from the Sun a planet is too cold to glow at SPHEREx's wavelengths, so all we could see is reflected sunlight.

These glow by their own warmth, mostly at 4 to 5 µm. Brightness comes from what our pipeline measured for the real object, scaled by distance.

How this works

Brightness. A planet shines by reflected sunlight. Twice as far from the Sun, it gets a quarter of the light, and we see a quarter of what it reflects back, so it looks 16 times fainter. A cold world between the stars shines by its own warmth, so it only fades with the square of its distance.

Motion. The pictures were taken months apart, from different places on Earth's orbit. A nearby planet seems to jump against the stars because we moved, not because it did. At a few hundred AU the jump between dates is bigger than the whole picture.

The limit. We measure the noise in each picture and call something visible when it is 5 times brighter than that noise. Between 3 and 5 times, you can only find it if you already know where to look.

What we simplified. The Sun is treated as a 5772 K glowing ball and the planet as evenly grey, on a circular orbit. The object is added as a smooth blob without its own noise, and pictures from one date are stacked, so you can't see it drift during a date. The cold worlds use our own pipeline's measurements of real brown dwarfs. Real searches add many more dates and much more care, and SPHEREx's all-sky maps get deeper as it keeps observing.
